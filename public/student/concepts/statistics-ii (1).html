Statistics II

Sampling · Margin of Error · Causation

This chapter is mostly conceptual — knowing exactly what a result does and doesn't let you conclude is the real skill. Formulas and principles first — including two SAT shortcuts not spelled out in this chapter — then worked examples, and finally practice questions from easy to extreme.

01 Formulas & Principles
Estimating a population total
\(\text{estimate}=\dfrac{\text{sample count}}{\text{sample size}}\times\text{population size}\)
200 of 1,000 sampled → \(\frac{200}{1000}\times18{,}000=3{,}600\)
Margin of error → plausible range
\(\text{plausible range}=\text{estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}\)
\(0.35\pm0.06\) → plausible proportion is between \(0.29\) and \(0.41\)
What margin of error applies to
It describes the population parameter — not individual members
Mean weight \(145\pm5\) lbs doesn't mean any one person is close to 145
Generalizability
Results generalize only to the population the sample was drawn FROM
A sample of pilots over 45 → conclusions apply only to pilots over 45
What affects margin of error
↑ variability → ↑ margin of error    ↑ sample size → ↓ margin of error
A bigger, more consistent sample gives a tighter, more trustworthy estimate
Sampling vs. assignment
Random sampling → generalizability   Random assignment → causation
Only a true experiment (random assignment to groups) can prove cause and effect
Association vs. causation
Association \(\neq\) Causation
Exercise linked to better grades \(\neq\) exercise causes better grades
Confounding variable
A hidden third variable that could explain the association
Age could explain an exercise–grades link if students weren't randomly assigned
Reading a line of best fit
\(y=mx+b\) — estimate \(m\) and \(b\) from the line itself
Use the line's predicted value, not the nearest actual data point
Association direction ↔ slope sign
Positive association \(\leftrightarrow\) positive slope; negative \(\leftrightarrow\) negative slope
A quick way to match a described trend to an equation's sign
SAT shortcut — not spelled out as its own rule in this chapter
Residual (actual − predicted)
\(\text{residual}=y_{\text{actual}}-y_{\text{predicted}}\)
The book never names it, but "actual vs. predicted" questions are exactly this
SAT shortcut — the formal term isn't used in this chapter
